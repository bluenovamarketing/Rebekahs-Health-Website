Making Sense of Sunscreen Ingredients

Wellness coaches Maddie and Victoria unpack the difference between mineral and chemical sunscreens—and the label details worth bringing into your decision.

Longer Michigan days often mean more time outside. Sun protection is one part of enjoying it thoughtfully, but crowded labels and competing claims can make choosing a sunscreen feel harder than it needs to be.

In this conversation, Maddie and Victoria—wellness coaches at our Lapeer store—compare the two broad sunscreen categories and offer a practical place to begin. This article is educational and does not replace advice from a dermatologist or other qualified healthcare professional.

Start with a sunscreen you can use consistently, then read the active-ingredient panel and directions—not just the marketing on the front.

What are the main types of sunscreen?

Victoria: Sunscreen active ingredients are often discussed in two groups: mineral filters and organic—or “chemical”—filters. Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide and/or titanium dioxide. Other formulas use filters such as avobenzone, octisalate, octocrylene, or homosalate.

Formulas can feel different on skin, and individual preferences vary. The label tells you the active ingredients, SPF, whether the product is broad spectrum, water-resistance timing, and directions for application and reapplication.

What should I look for on the label?

Maddie: Look beyond a single ingredient. Check that the product is labeled broad spectrum, choose an appropriate SPF for your plans, and follow the application directions. If you’ll be swimming or sweating, note the water-resistance time.

A simple label-reading checklist

  • Broad spectrum: indicates UVA and UVB protection under the product’s labeled conditions of use.
  • SPF: follow current public-health guidance and your clinician’s advice for your needs.
  • Active ingredients: identify which UV filters the formula uses.
  • Directions: check when and how often to apply, especially after swimming or sweating.
  • Age and sensitivity notes: ask a pediatrician about infants and a clinician about reactions or skin conditions.
A note on evolving guidance

Sunscreen regulation and evidence continue to develop. “Natural,” “clean,” and “reef-friendly” are not substitutes for reading the full label or checking current guidance for your health and destination.

How do I choose between formulas?

Victoria: Texture, finish, skin sensitivity, activity, and willingness to reapply can all matter. Some people prefer mineral formulas; others find a different approved sunscreen easier to wear consistently. If a product irritates your skin or you have specific medical concerns, talk with a dermatologist.

Sunscreen works best as one part of a broader sun-safety routine that can include shade, protective clothing, a wide-brimmed hat, and planning around intense midday sun.
